If standards (or more precisely, the drafters of those standards) could provide easy right answers, ethical
dilemmas would not be dilemmas. Dilemmas exist precisely because they are resistant to certain correct answers. There are
other reasons why it is notoriously difficult to draft standards which provide “right” answers. They include:
147
1. The inherent vagueness of language. 2. The traditional tensions between the need for generality on the one hand, and the need for specificity on the other. 3. The fact that no set of standards can possibly cover every eventuality that might arise in mediation. It is unrealistic
to expect them to do so. And in fact, it would be highly undesirable to produce a set of standards which purported
to provide an answer for every situation. Mediation is highly valued as a dynamic, flexible and informal process. The mediation community does not want or need a set of formal, inflexible rules. Mediators need room to exercise
discretion in relation to some matters. 4. The difficulty involved in gaining consensus from all stakeholders. Despite the challenges involved in drafting standards of conduct, it is possible for a set of standards to:
1. Alert mediators to the potential for value conflict. Section 9.7 of the old PS is a perfect example of such a
provision. Unfortunately, it has been omitted from the new PS.
